1. Introduction to Projectiles

Welcome! Today we are studying how objects fly through the air.

A projectile is any object thrown, shot, or launched that is subject only to the force of gravity.

💬 Class Discussion:

Look around the room or think about your morning. Can you name 3 examples of projectiles you have seen today?

2. The Launch Push

To become a projectile, an object needs an initial push. This gives it its Initial Velocity.

Once it leaves the cannon or your hand, you can no longer push it. It coasts forward purely on its own momentum!

💬 Class Discussion:

If you push a shopping cart really hard and let go, what happens? How is this similar to a cannonball leaving a cannon?

3. Gravity's Constant Pull

While the initial force pushes the object forward, Gravity is constantly pulling it straight down.

On Earth, gravity accelerates objects downward at $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$. This means every second it falls, it falls faster.

💬 Class Discussion:

If I drop a heavy bowling ball and a light tennis ball from the same height, which one hits the ground first? Why?

4. Two Motions in One

The coolest part of physics: Horizontal (forward) and Vertical (downward) motions are independent!

The forward speed doesn't care about gravity, and gravity doesn't care about the forward speed.

💬 Class Discussion:

If you drop a bullet from your hand, and fire a bullet perfectly horizontally from a gun at the exact same time, which one hits the ground first?

5. The Parabolic Arc

When you combine constant forward motion with accelerating downward motion, you get a special geometric curve.

This shape is called a Parabola. It is perfectly symmetrical in a vacuum.

💬 Class Discussion:

Where else in the real world have you seen this exact U-shaped curve?

6. Launch Angle Basics

The angle you point your cannon changes the shape of the parabola entirely.

  • High Angles (e.g., 75°): High hang time, short distance.
  • Low Angles (e.g., 15°): Fast and flat, hits the ground quickly.

💬 Class Discussion:

Who usually throws at a higher angle: A pitcher throwing a strike, or an outfielder throwing to home plate?

7. The 45° Rule

In physics (ignoring air), 45 Degrees gives you the maximum horizontal distance (range).

It is the perfect 50/50 split between staying in the air long enough, and moving forward fast enough.

💬 Class Discussion:

If you want to win a long-jump competition, at what angle should you push off the ground?

8. Complementary Angles

Here is a neat trick: Angles that add up to 90° (complementary angles) will land in the exact same spot!

  • 30° and 60° hit the same target.
  • 20° and 70° hit the same target.

💬 Class Discussion:

If a 30° and a 60° shot land in the exact same place, what is the difference between the two shots?

9. Real World Air Resistance

Everything we just learned is true in a vacuum. But on Earth, we have air.

Air Resistance (Drag) pushes back against the projectile, slowing it down. Real parabolas aren't perfectly symmetrical.

💬 Class Discussion:

How does throwing a crumpled piece of paper compare to throwing a flat sheet of paper? Why?

10. Peak Height (The Apex)

The highest point of a projectile's flight is called the Apex.

At the exact moment the projectile hits the apex, it stops moving up and is about to start falling down.

💬 Class Discussion:

What is the object's vertical speed at the very top of its arc?

11. Hang Time

Hang Time is the total amount of time a projectile stays in the air.

The only thing that controls how long something stays in the air is how high it goes!

💬 Class Discussion:

In a football game, does the punter want maximum distance, maximum hang time, or a balance of both? Why?

12. Does Mass Matter?

In a perfect vacuum, mass does not affect the path of a projectile. A feather and a hammer fall at the exact same rate!

💬 Class Discussion:

If we fire a 10 lb cannonball and a 100 lb cannonball using the exact same power and angle, will they land in the same spot? (Assume no air!)

13. Designing for Drag

Because air resistance is real, engineers design things to be aerodynamic so they slice through the air and maintain their velocity.

💬 Class Discussion:

How would deploying a parachute change the shape of our parabola midway through the flight?

14. Projectiles in Sports

Every time a ball is in the air during a game, it's a projectile obeying the exact rules we are learning today.

💬 Class Discussion:

Think about a basketball free throw. Why do coaches tell players to put more "arc" (a higher angle) on their shot?

15. The Effect of Wind

Wind adds an external force in the horizontal direction. It can speed a projectile up (tailwind) or slow it down (headwind).

💬 Class Discussion:

If you are playing our cannon game, and a strong wind is blowing toward you, how should you adjust your next shot?

16. Firing from an Elevation

If you shoot a cannon off a high cliff, the parabola is cut off at the start, but extended at the end as it falls below the launch height.

💬 Class Discussion:

If you are standing on a tall building, is 45° still the best angle for maximum distance, or should it be lower/higher?

17. Escape Velocity

If you shoot a projectile fast enough, the Earth curves away beneath it at the exact same rate it falls. This is an orbit!

If you shoot it even faster, it completely breaks free of gravity. This is Escape Velocity.

💬 Class Discussion:

How fast do you think a rocket has to travel to escape Earth's gravity entirely?

18. Reviewing the Rules

Let's recap what we learned before we go to the game:

  • X and Y motions are totally independent.
  • Gravity accelerates objects downwards steadily.
  • A 45° angle generally provides the furthest distance.
  • Complementary angles hit the same target (high vs flat).

💬 Class Discussion:

Are there any questions about how projectiles work before we move on?

In our game, the landscape will change every time someone scores.

You will have to constantly adjust your Angle and Power to adapt to the new mountain barrier.

💬 Class Discussion:
