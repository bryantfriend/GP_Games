Part 1: What is research?

Finding new information to answer a question.

Why do we research?

To Learn

We want to understand something new.

To Check Facts

We want to see if something is true.

To Argue

We want to prove a point with evidence.

Part 2: The Rules

How to spot a good question.

Rule 1: Not too simple

If you can answer the question with a quick Google search, it's too simple.

Bad: "What is the capital of France?"

Good: "How did Paris become a cultural capital?"

Rule 2: Not too big

You don't have time to write a 1,000-page book! Pick a focused topic.

Bad: "Why do wars happen?"

Good: "What caused the start of WWI?"

Rule 3: Creates debate

A good question has more than one right answer. People can disagree.

Bad: "Are dogs animals?"

Good: "Are dogs better pets than cats?"

Remember the Rules:

Not too simple

Not too big

Creates debate

Part 3: Let's Practice!

Look at the questions in your book.

Test Knowledge: Question A

"When do children start school?"

Too simple!

  • Answer is just a number.
  • No debate possible.

How can we fix Question A?

"When do children start school?"

"Is it better for children to start school at age 4 or age 6?"

(Discuss with class: Why is this better?)

Test Knowledge: Question C

"What is poverty?"

Too big!

Needs a whole book, not one project.

How can we fix Question C?

"What is poverty?"

"How does child poverty affect test scores in our city?"

(Discuss with class: We made it focused!)

Test Knowledge: Question D

"Why are social networking sites bad?"

Unfair question!

Only looks at bad things. No balance.

Summary Checklist

Before you start your project, check your question:

  • Is it focused? (Not too big)
  • Does it need research? (Not too simple)
  • Is it fair and balanced?
  • Can people debate it?
